i did not build learnsol because i had a clever growth plan sitting in a notes app. i built it because learning solana felt more confusing than it needed to be. the docs were scattered, the explanations assumed you had already crossed half the bridge, and beginners were often handed a roadmap when what they needed was one obvious place to start.

that part was clear to me. the less clear part was how anyone would find it.

people talk about launching a product as if the launch button is the hard part. launch day gives you a different problem: sitting there afterwards and realising nobody is obligated to care. your product can be useful. your landing page can look clean. you can have spent months making every small detail feel right. none of that creates attention on its own.

before learnsol existed, i had already spent time sharing what i was learning around solana. rust mistakes, small wins, things i did not understand, things that clicked five minutes later, and the occasional meme when i had nothing intelligent left to say. i was not building a personal brand in the loud, cringe way people usually mean it. i was leaving a trail.

when learnsol finally had something worth showing, some people already knew what i had been trying to make. they had seen the problem before the product had a name. the launch had a place to land.

between october 7, 2025 and august 9, 2026, clarity recorded 3,225 sessions from 2,193 unique users on learnsol. 493 of those sessions were bots, so i am not going to sit here and call every number a loyal user or a life changed. 170 sessions included a sign-up event. google sent 560 sessions. t.co sent 510.

the numbers show that the product had made it beyond my laptop.

the first few posts after launch did not make the internet stand up and applaud. nothing happened like that. i shared the product, then i shared a new module, then a bug, then a screenshot, then a thing someone tried, then another change. it was repetitive in the way anything real is repetitive. you do not post once and receive an audience from a vending machine. you keep talking until people have enough context to care.

most people disappear too early. they make something, announce it once, check the analytics twelve times, then decide the idea has failed because the first day was quiet. i get it. silence makes you feel stupid. it feels like you made the thing in public and everyone walked past it.

but a quiet day does not tell you much. sometimes your product is bad. sometimes the message is bad. sometimes the people who would care have not seen it yet because you gave them one chance to notice and then went offline to protect your ego.

learnsol started moving because i kept giving people a reason to come back. there were new pages, new challenges, and a clearer path through the material. modules accounted for 661 sessions, challenges accounted for 653, and the rust challenge path saw 394. people kept looking for what came next.

that was the only signal i needed.

the product had to do its part too. distribution can get someone through the door, but it cannot make a confusing thing useful. it cannot make a beginner understand an accounts model after one tweet. if the next step is vague, people leave. if the product asks them to open fourteen tabs before they feel any progress, they leave even faster.

i kept coming back to a very simple question: if someone lands here knowing almost nothing, what can they do in the next five minutes that makes them feel less lost?

that question shaped more of learnsol than any growth advice did. start somewhere obvious. try something small. get a win. then see the next thing. the average session reached 2.29 pages and average scroll depth was 77.67%. those numbers do not prove that everyone became a solana engineer. they tell me people were willing to spend more than ten seconds deciding the product was not for them.

there is a weird temptation to turn all of this into a neat little flywheel diagram. share the work, get users, make the product better, get more users. it is true, but it is also boring when you are the one doing it. you are usually tired. you do not know whether the next post will do anything. you are fixing something small while wondering if you should be working on something bigger. you are learning that consistency sounds romantic until it is 11pm and nobody asked you to do any of this.

still, that is where the compounding happened.

the compounding came from showing up before launch, showing up after launch, and making sure the product had something real behind the attention when it arrived.

if you are building something, do not wait until it is finished before you let people see the work. you do not need to perform expertise. you need to be visible enough that the right people can recognise the problem you are trying to solve.

then make the first experience good enough that they do not regret clicking.

that is all learnsol did. it gave people a place to begin, and i kept giving it reasons to stay alive.
